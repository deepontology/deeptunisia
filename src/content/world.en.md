{#how-read}

This table lists every counterparty Tunisia has a **measured** relationship with — a trade or debt figure — or a **claimed** one: a treaty, or a corporate tie the graph records. The two kinds are different objects and the table does not dress them alike.

- A **measurement** (trade, energy, debt) shows the publisher and the retrieval date, never a confidence grade. No human here checked forty thousand numbers; the honesty is in saying where each one came from.
- A **claim** (a treaty, a company relationship) carries its grade — basis, confidence, sources — and opens the same record the network opens.
- An **em dash** means the year carries no observation. It is never a zero: zero would assert that trade was measured and found to be none.
- All figures are **current US dollars, millions** — not inflation-adjusted. A billion dollars in 1975 is not a billion dollars now; the year-by-year series makes that visible, but no number here is corrected for it.

The globe draws the **shape** — the top twenty partners, so the structure stays legible. This table has **every row**. That division of labour is the point: geography for structure, the ledger for completeness.

{#explain-trade}

Bilateral goods trade, from UN Comtrade, 1962 to the last reporting year. **Exports** are what Tunisia shipped to the partner; **imports** are what Tunisia bought. Two statistical offices describe every shipment — Tunisia's own filings, and the partner's mirror account — and they disagree, routinely and substantially: exports are valued free-on-board, imports cost-insurance-freight; shipments cross the year boundary; goods re-routed through a third country are attributed to whoever last handled them. The two figures are shown side by side, never averaged, and the **gap** column states how far apart they are. A persistent, one-sided gap is usually telling you how the trade is actually routed — it is a finding, not noise.

What this does **not** contain: services (tourism, engineering work, IT contracting), which no redistributable bilateral dataset covers, and the partner aggregates Comtrade itself cannot place ("Areas, nes" and similar), which is why the sum of the rows is smaller than the publisher's total.

{#explain-debt}

External public and publicly guaranteed debt by creditor, from the World Bank's International Debt Statistics, 1970 to the last reported stock year. **Owed** is the stock — what is owed right now, a level. **Received** and **Repaid** are the year's movements: money arriving, and principal plus interest going back out. For most of the period since the 1990s the outbound number is the larger one, which is why the two are kept apart rather than netted.

Two boundaries matter. First, this is **external** debt: domestic borrowing — which since 2024 has exceeded the external stock — is not in this series. Second, it is **public** debt: private companies' foreign borrowing is absent. The strip's totals carry the same boundaries; the dossier's shelf links the aggregate figures that include domestic debt.

Creditors that are not countries — the World Bank, the African Development Bank, bondholders — appear in the **Bodies & markets** section. A bondholder is not somewhere; it gets a row, never a dot on the globe.

{#explain-energy}

Energy trade split by fuel — crude oil, refined products and natural gas — from the same Comtrade series, matched across the two commodity classifications at the 1988 changeover. The split matters more than a total would: crude and refined product are bought on a world market, while gas comes down a pipe from one neighbour. Tunisia exported over a billion dollars of crude in 1980 and is now a net energy importer; scrubbing the time cursor across that reversal is the clearest single argument this view makes.

{#explain-totals}

The totals row and the strip above use the **publisher's own aggregate** — Comtrade's world total, the World Bank's world counterpart — not the sum of the rows. The two differ for a reason: the build discards aggregates no country can be identified for ("Areas, nes" and friends), so the sum of the rows is smaller than the total by exactly that amount. Where a snapshot predates the totals series, the table shows the sum of its rows and marks it with Σ.

The **balance** is derived — exports minus imports — and is labelled as a derivation, not a measurement. The same rule applies to every derived number in this view: it says what it is made of.

{#dossier-claims}

This shelf is not a verdict. It records claims that circulate about a relationship, names who makes them, and places the measured or documented counter-evidence beside them. A low-confidence claim stays visibly low-confidence; it never becomes a fact because it appears next to a number.

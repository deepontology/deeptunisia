# How to read this article

Each date, figure and event in the text carries a small chip. Open it and you get the claim as we state it, its grade, and the sources behind it. The grades describe the kind of source, not how persuasive the story is.

- **documented**: a gazette, a decree, a court record or a constitutional text [C016]
- **reported**: press, an NGO, a UN body or testimony, attributed to whoever said it [C006]
- **unsubstantiated**: circulating but not established, kept visible with its holder named [C037]

Where we say what the evidence adds up to, the reading is set in a marked panel: who holds it, why, and what would prove it wrong. Those panels are our inferences. They are not findings, and nothing in them is graded.

<section_id: S01>
title: "A letter marked no address"
type: narrative
---

In July 2001 a judge in Tunis wrote to the president. Mokhtar Yahyaoui, presiding judge of the tenth chamber of the Court of First Instance, told @ben-ali that the judiciary was no longer independent, and that judges were handing down rulings dictated by the political power, under fear, suspicion and denunciation [C004]. He sent it to the presidency in Carthage by ordinary post. It came back to him marked no address [C005].

The judge was suspended eight days after the date of the letter and removed from the bench that December [C006]. The letter itself found another road. His nephew Zouhair Yahyaoui published it on TUNeZINE, an opposition site [C007]. In June 2002 six plainclothes officers arrested Zouhair in the internet café in Ben Arous where he worked, and a court sentenced him to two years on a charge of spreading false news [C008]. He went on hunger strike, came out in 2003 and died in 2005 at the age of 37 [C009].

Twenty years later, in September 2022, the president signed Decree-Law 54. Its Article 24 punished spreading false news over communication networks with five years in prison [C080]. In October 2025 a court sentenced a 51-year-old man to death over Facebook posts, with that article among the charges [C105][C106]. The same charge, the same kind of arrest, and between them one institution that appears in both stories: the Ministry of the Interior, in a building on Avenue Habib Bourguiba in the middle of Tunis [C001]. Photographs show masts, dishes and antennas on its roof [C002], and a UN working group has recorded a detainee held in a holding cell in its basement [C003].

In Tunisian Derja the word for ruler and the word for the police officer who stops you in the street are the same: el hakem. People say el hakem is here, and mean the police. The Interior Ministry is, in everyday speech, the ministry of el hakem. That is usage, not a sourced claim, and we cite nothing for it.

<section_id: S02>
title: "The claim, and how it could be wrong"
type: narrative
---

This article makes one argument. Since 1987, whoever has ruled Tunisia has ruled it through the Ministry of the Interior, and the evidence for that is in appointments, decrees and court records that anyone can check. Max Weber defined the state as the claim to the monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force within a territory [C010]. In Tunisia, that monopoly has an address [C011].

The evidence points to five findings.

- Ben Ali ran the ministry through men who stayed for years, and attached his own presidential guard to the presidency in 1988 [C015][C016].
- When he fell, the ruling party was dissolved by a court and the ministry was not. An announced dissolution of the political police was never followed by a gazetted text [C034][C036].
- For the next ten years no one held the building for long: at least eleven directors general of the national police between 2011 and July 2021 [C045].
- In 2021 the president took it, in a sequence of speech, suspension of parliament, new commanders and a decree that closed the courts to challenges [C059][C061][C068][C069].
- Since then the commanders have stayed while the ministers rotate, and the institutions that once restrained the police have each been dismantled or turned against their own members [C130][C074].

> [I02]

The argument would be wrong if a ruler since the takeover of 7 November 1987 [C013] had lost control of the ministry and kept power, or if its leadership had changed without the president's leave. We looked for such a case and did not find one. That is a statement about our search, not a proof, and it is the reason this article is written as a claim that can fail.

The argument does not say the two eras are identical. Ben Ali had a mass party and Tunisia's president today has none [C019][C078]. It does not say that every act described here was ordered from one desk. And it does not rest on the film that accompanies it. Everything below stands on its own sources.

<section_id: S03>
title: "How Ben Ali built it"
type: narrative
---

Zine El Abidine Ben Ali did not come from parliament or the parties. He came from the security services: more than ten years in military security, twice at the head of the national police, interior minister from 28 April 1986 [C012]. On 7 November 1987 he took the presidency on the strength of a medical report, signed by seven doctors, declaring Bourguiba unfit [C013].

His method was stability at the top of the ministry. @ali-seriati became head of the national police in 1991 [C014]. On 1 September 2001 he moved to head presidential security and stayed until the day Ben Ali left, 14 January 2011 [C015]. @mohamed-ali-ganzoui was secretary of state for security from 1995 to 2002 [C018]. And in February 1988, Decree 88-250 attached presidential security to the presidency itself, out of the ministry [C016]. @abderrahmane-belhaj-ali headed the president's security [C017].

> [I03]

Two pillars supplied the eyes. The ruling party, the RCD, claimed more than two million members and thousands of local cells [C019]. The ministry had about 100,000 uniformed police, and within it the Directorate of State Security, the political police [C020].

The pillars were turned on the institutions that could have resisted. In 1991, 244 military men were arrested over an alleged coup plot, the Barraket Essahel affair. The victims say the charge was fabricated, and they and Human Rights Watch say many were tortured in the basements of the Interior Ministry. @abdallah-kallel was minister at the time [C021].

> [I04]

Courts and law followed. In the summer of 1992 military courts tried 279 people accused of belonging to Ennahda and convicted 265, in trials Amnesty International said broke fair-trial standards [C022]. In 2002 a referendum removed the limit on presidential terms and added Article 41 of the 1959 constitution, which gave the president immunity for acts done in office [C023]. In 2003 a counter-terrorism law defined terrorism broadly enough to reach disturbing public order [C024]. Official figures count 1,123 prosecutions under it from 2004 to 2009, and a UN estimate cited by Amnesty reaches about 3,000 [C025].

> [I05]

<section_id: S04>
title: "2011: the ruler fell, the building stayed"
type: narrative
---

On 14 January 2011 Tunisians gathered in front of the Interior Ministry [C026]. That evening Seriati escorted Ben Ali and his family to the foot of the plane. Twenty-seven minutes later, according to Jeune Afrique, he was arrested on the order of the defence minister, who suspected a coup plot [C027][C028].

The accounting that followed was uneven. In July 2012 a military court in Tunis sentenced Seriati to twenty years in the case of the revolution's martyrs [C029]. On appeal in April 2014 the sentence was cut to three years, and he walked free on 17 May [C030][C031]. Kallel, interior minister from 1991 to 1995 [C032], was sentenced to four years, halved on appeal in April 2012 [C033].

The party and the ministry met different fates. On 9 March 2011 the Tunis court dissolved the RCD and ordered its assets liquidated [C034]. Two days earlier, an Interior Ministry spokesman had said the political police and State Security were dissolved [C035]. According to Nawaat, no legal text followed in the Official Gazette [C036]. In early March Abdessattar Bennour was appointed director general of the national police, the same post he had held in 2005 [C043][C044].

> [I06]

### Two stories that do not hold

Two accounts from those weeks circulate widely and should be set aside. There was talk of snipers firing on protesters [C037], but a member of the fact-finding commission said there was not enough credible testimony to prove it [C038]. And a rumour spread that General @rachid-ammar had refused Ben Ali's order to fire [C039]. It began with a Facebook post, and its author, Yassine Ayari, admitted in July 2011 that he made it up [C040]. Ammar told the military court under oath that no such order ever reached him [C041]. We found no prosecution of Ayari for it [C042].

<section_id: S05>
title: "A decade in which nobody could own it"
type: narrative
---

Between the revolution and July 2021 we counted at least eleven directors general of the national police [C045]. Réalités counts twelve interior ministers from 2011 to January 2021 [C046]. The post itself was abolished in March 2015 and restored that December, when Abderrahmane Belhaj Ali, once Ben Ali's head of presidential security, was brought in to fill it. He resigned a year later [C047][C048][C049].

The police organised. The first mobilisations for a union date from 15 January 2011, and Decree-Law 2011-42 followed on 31 May [C050][C051]. The main union claimed 62,000 members and Crisis Group estimated about 8,000 [C052]. On 26 February 2018 the unions' weight showed at the Ben Arous court: five officers accused of torture were being questioned, three were put in detention, and a crowd of police, some armed, gathered outside. According to the prosecution, the three were released that evening [C053][C054].

> [I07]

The parties fought for the building too. Under Article 89 of the 2014 constitution the president asks the party or coalition with the most seats to form the government, so the road to the ministry ran through parliament [C055]. @lotfi-brahem became interior minister in September 2017 and was dismissed in June 2018 [C056]. In January 2021 @hichem-mechichi dismissed Taoufik Charfeddine and cancelled his appointments and transfers in the police and the Guard [C057].

Reform was promised and mostly not delivered. In November 2015 the European Union set aside €23 million for a security-reform programme. About €15 million went to border command centres. The independent ethics body that was meant to take citizens' complaints, and a code of conduct, were not created before the programme closed at the end of 2020 [C058].

> [I08]

---

<section_id: S06>
title: "July 2021: someone takes it"
type: narrative
---

The first signal came in April. At the Internal Security Forces Day ceremony, with the head of government and the speaker of parliament present, @kais-saied said the president is the supreme commander of the armed forces, military and civilian [C059]. The 2014 constitution has articles on the president and the government in security matters, but none that explicitly places the police under either [C060].

> [I09]

On 25 July Saied invoked Article 80, froze parliament and dismissed the head of government [C061]. The Bardo was sealed by the army, not the police, with an armoured vehicle at the gate [C062]. During the night the speaker, @rached-ghannouchi, was kept out. A video shows a deputy speaker telling an officer they are guardians of the constitution, and the officer answering that they are guardians of the nation, following orders [C063].

That night the Interior Ministry had no minister, because Mechichi had held it himself since January [C064]. Tunisie Tribune reported that colonel major @khaled-yahyaoui, head of presidential security, took command of it [C065]. The next day plainclothes police closed Al Jazeera's Tunis office; the officers said they carried judicial orders and the journalists said no warrant was shown [C066].

> [I10]

On 29 July Ridha Gharsallaoui, a police commissioner who had become the president's national security adviser, was put in charge of the ministry [C067]. On 18 August the presidency replaced the heads of the national police and the National Guard [C068]. On 22 September Decree 2021-117 provided that legislation would be issued as decree-laws, which cannot be challenged for annulment [C069].

> [I11]

<section_id: S07>
title: "What control looks like"
type: narrative
---

The rotation of the previous decade stopped at the top of the two forces. @mourad-saidane took up the national police on 31 May 2022, and La Presse still described him in the role in October 2025 [C070]. The National Guard changed hands in July 2022 and May 2023, and Hossein Gharbi, who has commanded it since, was presented as its current commander in September 2026 [C071][C130]. Above them, Khaled Yahyaoui has directed presidential security since 2019 [C072]. In June 2024 Africa Intelligence headlined him as the security chief who makes and breaks ministers, which is press reporting, not an official statement [C073].

The ministers, meanwhile, rotate. Five have held the ministry since July 2021 [C074]. On the day the fifth, @khaled-nouri, took office in May 2024, a post that existed under Ben Ali came back: secretary of state to the interior minister for the national police, given to Sofiane Bessadok. Under Ben Ali the same post was held by Ganzoui [C075]. And the presidency has published readouts of meetings that put the president, the minister and the two commanders in one room [C076].

<section_id: S08>
title: "Five institutions that could say no"
type: narrative
---

If the party is gone, the question is what holds the system in place. Our reading is that it rests on five institutions that once limited the police and no longer can. Their treatment is easier to follow one by one, but the first thing to see is how closely they were struck. In a single week in September 2022, Decree-Law 54 was published [C080], the provisional judicial council lifted the immunity of the president of the judges' association [C081], and the military court in Sfax issued warrants against eight police union leaders [C082].

> [I14]

### The judges

On the night of 5 February 2022 Saied declared on video that the Supreme Judicial Council was a thing of the past, and its seat was shut two days later [C083]. A decree-law dissolved it on 12 February [C084]. On 1 June the president gained the power to dismiss judges directly, and 57 judges and prosecutors were dismissed the same day [C085]. On 10 August the Administrative Court suspended 49 of the dismissals. The ruling was not carried out, and six months on none of the judges had been reinstated [C086]. On 22 February 2023 Saied told judges that whoever acquits the political detainees is their accomplice [C087].

The president of the judges' association, @anas-hmaidi, was sent to trial under Article 136 of the penal code [C088]. In July 2026 an appeal court upheld a one-year sentence against him, in absentia. His association says he left the country legally for an urgent medical condition; Judges for Judges says he left before the verdict [C089].

> [I15]

### The police unions

On 29 August 2022 Charfeddine stopped the automatic deduction of union dues, worth forty million dinars a year by his own estimate [C090]. The unions held a sit-in in Sfax, forces removed the tent with tear gas, and a military court issued the warrants [C091]. In March 2023 a code of conduct finally arrived, the one the reform years never produced [C092]. In October 2025 the former secretary general of the main union was sentenced to four years in a case over union funds [C093].

> [I16]

### Lawyers and civil society

On 11 May 2024 plainclothes officers arrested the lawyer Sonia Dahmani at the Maison de l'Avocat, live on France 24, over a remark on Carthage Plus [C094]. Days later police entered the same building again and arrested the lawyer Mehdi Zagrouba. The ICJ page says he reportedly showed physical signs of torture, his lawyers say he lost consciousness, and the ministry denies any mistreatment [C095]. We found no published outcome of an investigation into his complaint [C096].

In April 2026 the Tunisian League for Human Rights, part of the quartet that won the 2015 Nobel Peace Prize, was notified of a one-month suspension. It called the decision arbitrary and went to court [C097].

### Military justice

In the three months after 25 July 2021 Amnesty recorded at least ten civilians before military courts, against six cases from 2011 to 2018 [C098]. Yassine Ayari, who invented the army story in 2011, went to prison on 28 July 2021 to serve a 2018 military sentence over Facebook posts [C099]. In December 2023 a military court gave the opposition figure @chaima-issa a one-year suspended sentence [C100].

### The speech law

By the end of 2023 Human Rights Watch counted about twenty people under criminal investigation under Decree-Law 54 [C101]. The journalists' union counted 28 files in January 2026 [C102]. Sonia Dahmani left prison on 27 November 2025 and was sentenced again in May 2026 to two years over remarks about prisons [C103]. The same month the journalist @zied-el-heni received a year under the Telecommunications Code, not the decree, for remarks at a seminar [C104].

The furthest case so far is Saber Ben Chouchane, 51, arrested in January 2024 and sentenced to death in October 2025 over Facebook posts [C105]. One charge was false news under Article 24 [C106]. He was pardoned on 7 October, and Human Rights Watch called it the first known death sentence in Tunisia over peaceful expression [C107].

### The cases at the top

The state-security conspiracy cases reached the opposition leadership. Arrests began in February 2023, and Rached Ghannouchi was arrested on 17 April [C108]. Inkyfada wrote that the accused were questioned on the basis of anonymous testimonies coded XX and XXX [C109]. In September 2026 the Court of Cassation rejected the final appeals, with sentences of up to 45 years [C110]. The building's own people are not exempt: @kamel-guizani, a former director general of the national police, was sentenced in absentia to 35 years in July 2025 [C111].

### At street level

All of this reaches one body, the police on the street. The code of conduct bans torture outright [C112], but we found no published case of an officer sanctioned under it [C113]. The World Organisation Against Torture documented 21 police assaults from June 2024 to May 2025 [C114]. In the case of Omar Abidi, who drowned in 2018 while being chased by police, an appeal court in July 2024 sentenced twelve officers to one year, suspended [C115].

<section_id: S09>
title: "Where it stands, autumn 2026"
type: narrative
---

The pressure has not eased. On 4 September 2026 Mohamed Yousfi, editor-in-chief of Al Katiba, was arrested, and a detention order followed on 8 September. Inkyfada reports the financial charges rest on a Facebook post and a comment, and that his name is not in them [C116]. After a demonstration for the Sumud flotilla activists, about fifteen people were arrested on 20 and 21 September, among them a minor [C117].

There is also movement the other way. On 30 September the Official Gazette amended Article 24 of Decree-Law 54: a fine of 10,000 to 20,000 dinars, with prison only for repeat offences [C118]. We count that in the objections below.

Detention is where the pressure is hardest to see. In the summer, AFP reported that at least 23 prisoners had reportedly died of heat. The authorities replied that no death can be attributed to heat without forensic confirmation, and published no figure [C119]. The League counts more than twenty, and an investigating judge took sixteen files [C120]. The businessman Lazhar Sta died in detention on 8 September, and his daughter alleges medical neglect. @abir-moussi's lawyer says she has been on hunger strike since 23 September, and Ahmed Nejib Chebbi says in a statement that he has been since 1 October [C121].

> [I17]

On 8 October Saied said that accountability is a legitimate demand and that the truth will come out with no immunity for anyone [C122]. Article 110 of the 2022 constitution says the president cannot be held accountable for acts done in the course of his duties [C123].

<section_id: S10>
title: "Is this a police state? The case and the objections"
type: narrative
---

Our answer is yes, with limits we will state.

> [I18]

The distance between the two eras is visible in one comparison. In 2002 a young man was jailed on a false-news charge. In 2025 a man was sentenced to death over Facebook posts [C124].

### The strongest objections

**The old apparatus was bigger on paper.** Ben Ali's party claimed more than two million members and thousands of cells, and the ministry about 100,000 uniformed police [C019][C020]. We have no comparable current figures for either, and we make no claim that today's apparatus is larger by those measures.

**The record is not one-directional.** The speech law was softened on 30 September [C118]. Ben Chouchane was pardoned [C107]. The president has said no one is above accountability [C122]. We read these against Article 110, which says the president cannot be held accountable for acts done in the course of his duties [C123], but a reader may weigh them differently.

**The links between events are inferred.** We found no document tying the three decisions of September 2022 to one another [C080][C081][C082], and we do not claim a chain of orders.

**Several key points rest on press reporting.** That Yahyaoui took command of the ministry that night, and what he does in Carthage today, come from single press reports graded C [C065][C127][C073]. We have not found a decree or an official statement that settles either.

**The decade before 2021 was contested, not owned.** Parties, unions and ministers fought over the building, and for ten years no one held it [C045][C053][C055]. The police state we describe is a feature of the period after 2021, not of the whole post-revolution era.

### What control buys

Control of the building matters because of what it protects. The 2022 constitution limits the presidency to two terms in Article 90, but the 2014 lock on amendments that extend terms was removed [C125]. A decree of 30 September 2026 lets the president choose three members of the commission that oversees the 2029 elections [C126].

> [I19]

Nobody holds the building alone, and one name stands out: Khaled Yahyaoui, who by Tunisie Tribune's account took command of the ministry on the night of 25 July 2021 and who, according to Mondafrique in August 2026, was still head of presidential security [C127].

> [I20]

In 2011 the people brought down the head of the regime [C128].

<section_id: S12>
title: "What this article leaves out"
type: narrative
---

The evidence behind this article was originally assembled for a film. Some statements in the film's voice are not carried by the sources in our files, and each was left out here.

- Yahyaoui's promotion to general in November 2021. We could not find it.
- The charge named for Seriati's reduced sentence, and the reason the torture charges against Kallel were dropped.
- The penal-code articles named for Ben Chouchane beyond Article 24.
- A claim about record heat and power cuts in summer 2026, beyond the AFP report on prisoners.
- The 13 May date for the second raid on the Maison de l'Avocat.
- The stamp on the returned letter: sources say it came back marked no address, and none mentions a stamp.

The film also gives 31 May 2022 for the decree appointing Saidane. That is the day he took office, and the decree itself is dated 17 June [C070].

# How to read this article

This is the written companion to the film The Ministry of El Hakem. It follows the film's argument, but it is not a transcript. It is built from the same evidence, and it holds itself to a stricter rule than the film's voice does: where the film said something that the sources do not carry, this text leaves it out and says so in the closing section.

Every figure and every dated event carries a grade and a source [C001]. A grade is a statement about the kind of source, not about how much we like the story.

- **documented**: a gazette, a decree, a court record or a constitutional text [C016]
- **reported**: press, an NGO, a UN body or testimony, attributed to who said it [C006]
- **unsubstantiated**: circulating but not established, kept visible with its holder named [C037]

Readings are set apart. Wherever the text says what the evidence adds up to, it appears as a marked panel with who holds the reading, why, and what would prove it wrong. Those panels are the authors' inferences. They are not findings.

<section_id: S01>
title: "The word and the letter"
type: narrative
---

On Avenue Habib Bourguiba, in the middle of Tunis, stands the Ministry of the Interior [C001]. Photographs show masts, dishes and antennas on the roof [C002]. A UN working group has recorded a detainee held in a basement holding cell [C003]. Our own rough measurement from satellite images puts the footprint at roughly six to eight thousand square metres and the height at about seven floors. Those are estimates, not survey figures.

In Tunisian Derja the word el hakem has two meanings. It is the ruler in the palace, and it is the police officer who stops you on the road. When someone says el hakem is here, they mean the police. The ministry, in popular speech, is the ministry of el hakem. That is everyday usage, not a sourced claim, and we cite no source for it.

In July 2001 a judge wrote to the president. Mokhtar Yahyaoui, presiding judge of the tenth chamber of the Tunis Court of First Instance, wrote to @ben-ali on 6 July that the judiciary was no longer independent and that judges were handing down rulings dictated by the political power, under fear, suspicion and denunciation [C004]. He sent it to the presidency by ordinary post. It came back marked no address [C005].

Eight days after the letter he was suspended, and on 29 December 2001 the disciplinary council removed him from the bench [C006]. By his own account he was then banned from travel for ten years and kept under watch [C006].

The letter found another road. It was published on TUNeZINE, an opposition site run by the judge's nephew Zouhair Yahyaoui [C007]. On 4 June 2002 six plainclothes officers arrested Zouhair in the internet café in Ben Arous where he worked. A court sentenced him to two years and four months, cut to two years on appeal, on a charge of spreading false news [C008]. He went on hunger strike, was released in November 2003, and died of a heart attack in March 2005, aged 37 [C009].

<section_id: S02>
title: "An address for the monopoly"
type: narrative
---

In 1919 Max Weber defined the state as the claim to the monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force within a territory [C010]. In Tunisia that monopoly has a street address: the building on Avenue Habib Bourguiba [C011].

> [I02]

<section_id: S03>
title: "Ben Ali's machine"
type: narrative
---

Zine El Abidine Ben Ali did not come from parliament or the parties. He came from inside the security apparatus: more than ten years in military security, twice at the head of the national police, interior minister from 28 April 1986 [C012]. On 7 November 1987 he took the presidency with a medical report signed by seven doctors declaring Bourguiba unfit [C013]. He knew the building well. That he knew it floor by floor is our inference, not a documented fact.

His answer to how to run it was the same men for a long time. @ali-seriati was named head of the national police in 1991 [C014]. On 1 September 2001 he moved to head presidential security and stayed until 14 January 2011 [C015]. That is close to twenty years at the top of the security command, under one president.

The design around him was consistent. In February 1988 Decree 88-250 attached presidential security to the presidency, out of the ministry [C016]. @abderrahmane-belhaj-ali headed the president's security [C017]. Inside the ministry, @mohamed-ali-ganzoui was secretary of state for security from 1995 to 2002 [C018].

> [I03]

The machine needed eyes. They came from two pillars. The ruling RCD claimed more than two million members and thousands of local cells [C019]. The ministry had about 100,000 uniformed police, and at its heart the Directorate of State Security, the political police [C020].

Not even the army was spared. In 1991, in the Barraket Essahel affair, 244 military men were arrested for an alleged coup plot. The victims say the charge was fabricated, and the victims and Human Rights Watch say many were tortured in the ministry's basements. @abdallah-kallel was interior minister at the time [C021].

> [I04]

The army's courts then became one more tool. In the summer of 1992 military courts tried 279 people accused of belonging to Ennahda and convicted 265. Amnesty International said the trials broke fair-trial standards [C022]. Ben Ali then wrote his own immunity into law: the 2002 referendum removed the limit on presidential terms and added Article 41 [C023]. In December 2003 Law 2003-75 defined terrorism broadly enough to reach disturbing public order [C024]. Official figures given to the UN rapporteur count 1,123 prosecutions between 2004 and 2009, and a UN estimate cited by Amnesty reaches about 3,000 [C025].

> [I05]

---

<section_id: S04>
title: "January 2011 and after"
type: narrative
---

On 14 January 2011 Tunisians gathered in front of the same building and shouted one word, dégage [C026]. That evening Seriati escorted Ben Ali and his family to the foot of the plane. Twenty-seven minutes later, according to Jeune Afrique, he was arrested [C027], on the order of defence minister Ridha Grira, who suspected a coup plot [C028].

Then came the trials. In July 2012 a military court in Tunis sentenced Seriati to twenty years [C029]. On appeal in April 2014 the sentence was cut to three years [C030], and on 17 May 2014 he walked free [C031]. Kallel, the interior minister of Barraket Essahel, was sentenced to four years, cut to two on appeal in April 2012 [C033].

The RCD fell fast: on 9 March 2011 the Tunis court dissolved it and ordered its assets liquidated [C034]. Two days earlier an Interior Ministry spokesman had said the political police and State Security were dissolved [C035]. According to Nawaat, no legal text followed in the Official Gazette [C036].

> [I06]

Two stories from those weeks should be set aside. There was talk of snipers firing on protesters [C037], but a member of the fact-finding commission said there was not enough credible testimony to prove it [C038]. And a rumour spread that General @rachid-ammar had refused Ben Ali's order to fire [C039]. It began with a Facebook post, and its author, Yassine Ayari, admitted in July 2011 that he made it up [C040]. Ammar told the military court, under oath, that no such order ever reached him [C041]. We found no prosecution of Ayari for it [C042].

In early March 2011, seven weeks after Ben Ali fell, Abdessattar Bennour was appointed director general of the national police [C043]. He had held the same post in 2005 [C044].

---

<section_id: S05>
title: "The decade of rotation"
type: narrative
---

Between the revolution and July 2021 we counted at least eleven directors general of the national police [C045]. Above them, Réalités counts twelve interior ministers from 2011 to January 2021 [C046]. The names churned, but the ministry kept going back to old faces. In March 2015 the post of director general was abolished [C047]. In December of that year secretary of state Rafik Chelly was removed and the post returned [C048]. The man brought in to hold it was Abderrahmane Belhaj Ali, who had headed Ben Ali's presidential security. He resigned a year later [C049].

The police also organised. The first mobilisations for a union date from 15 January 2011 [C050], and Decree-Law 2011-42 was published on 31 May 2011 [C051]. The main union claimed 62,000 members, Crisis Group estimated about 8,000 [C052]. Either way it was a force in the street.

On 26 February 2018, at the Ben Arous court, five officers accused of torture were questioned and three were placed in detention while a crowd of police gathered outside, some armed [C053]. According to the prosecution, the three were released that evening [C054].

> [I07]

Above the police, the parties fought over the ministry. Under Article 89 of the 2014 constitution the president asks the party or coalition with the most seats to form the government [C055], so the road to the building ran through parliament. Security appointments became a battleground. @lotfi-brahem was made interior minister in September 2017 and dismissed in June 2018 [C056]. In January 2021 @hichem-mechichi dismissed Taoufik Charfeddine and cancelled all his appointments and transfers in the police and the Guard [C057].

Reform was promised and not delivered. In November 2015 the EU set aside €23 million for the PARMSS programme. About €15 million went to border command centres, more than €1.5 million to an ethics body project, and neither the independent complaints body nor the code of conduct was created before the programme closed at the end of 2020 [C058].

> [I08]

---

<section_id: S06>
title: "25 July 2021"
type: narrative
---

On 18 April 2021, at Internal Security Forces Day, @kais-saied said the president is the supreme commander of the armed forces, military and civilian [C059]. The 2014 constitution has articles on the president and the government in security matters, but none that explicitly places the police under either [C060].

> [I09]

Three months later, on 25 July, Saied invoked Article 80, froze parliament and dismissed the head of government [C061]. The entrances of the Bardo were sealed by the army, not the police, and an armoured vehicle moved up to the gate [C062]. During the night @rached-ghannouchi was kept out, and a video shows deputy speaker Samira Chaouachi telling an officer they are guardians of the constitution and the officer answering that they are guardians of the nation, following orders [C063].

That night the ministry had no minister. Mechichi had been acting interior minister since January [C064]. A name appears that matters later: colonel major @khaled-yahyaoui, head of presidential security. Tunisie Tribune reported that he took command of the ministry [C065].

The next day plainclothes police closed Al Jazeera's Tunis office. The officers said they carried judicial orders, and the journalists said no warrant was shown [C066].

> [I10]

On 29 July Ridha Gharsallaoui, a police commissioner turned presidential national security adviser, was put in charge of the ministry [C067]. On 18 August the presidency changed the commanders of the national police and the National Guard, naming Sami Hichri and Chokri Riahi [C068]. On 22 September Decree 2021-117 provided that legislation would come as decree-laws that cannot be challenged for annulment [C069].

> [I11]

---

<section_id: S07>
title: "The faces stop changing"
type: narrative
---

From 2022 the rotation stopped. @mourad-saidane took up the national police on 31 May 2022 and was still described in the role in October 2025 [C070]. Hossein Gharbi has commanded the National Guard since May 2023 and was presented as its current commander in September 2026 [C071]. Above them, Khaled Yahyaoui has directed presidential security since 2019 [C072]. In June 2024 Africa Intelligence headlined him as the security chief who makes and breaks ministers, which is press reporting, not an official statement [C073].

The ministers kept rotating. Five have held the ministry since July 2021 [C074]. On the day @khaled-nouri came in, in May 2024, a post that existed under Ben Ali was brought back: secretary of state to the interior minister for the national police, given to Sofiane Bessadok. Under Ben Ali the same post was held by Ganzoui [C075]. The presidency has published readouts of meetings between the president, the minister and the two commanders [C076].

> [I12]

---

<section_id: S08>
title: "One week in September 2022"
type: narrative
---

Ben Ali had a party. Saied does not. In the December 2022 elections candidates ran without parties [C078], and first-round turnout was 8.8 percent at the close of polls, later given as a preliminary 11.22 percent [C079]. The RCD, for comparison, had claimed over two million members until a court dissolved it [C077].

If there is no party, what takes its place? Our reading is five arms.

> [I13]

To see them work together, look at one week. On 16 September 2022 Decree-Law 54 was published. Its Article 24 punished spreading false news on communication networks with five years in prison and a fine, doubled if the target is a public official [C080]. On 20 September the provisional judicial council lifted the immunity of @anas-hmaidi, the head of the judges' association [C081]. On 23 September the military court in Sfax issued warrants against eight police union leaders [C082].

> [I14]

---

<section_id: S09>
title: "First arm: the judges"
type: narrative
---

On the night of 5 February 2022 Saied declared on video that the Supreme Judicial Council was a thing of the past, and its seat was shut two days later [C083]. On 12 February a decree-law dissolved it [C084]. On 1 June the president gained the power to dismiss judges, and the same day 57 judges and prosecutors were dismissed [C085]. On 10 August the Administrative Court suspended the dismissal of 49 of them. The ruling was not carried out, and six months on, none had been reinstated [C086]. On 22 February 2023 Saied told judges that whoever acquits the political detainees is their accomplice [C087].

> [I15]

Even the heads of judges' bodies were not spared. Anas Hamadi was referred to trial under Article 136 of the penal code [C088]. In July 2026 an appeal court upheld a one-year sentence against him, in absentia. His association says he left the country legally for an urgent medical condition, and Judges for Judges says he left before the verdict [C089].

---

<section_id: S10>
title: "Second arm: the police unions"
type: narrative
---

On 29 August 2022 Charfeddine stopped the automatic deduction of union dues, worth forty million dinars a year by his own estimate [C090]. The unions staged a sit-in in Sfax. Forces removed the tent, with clashes and tear gas, and a military court issued warrants against eight leaders [C091]. In March 2023 a code of conduct finally arrived, the thing the reform years never delivered [C092]. In October 2025 the former secretary general of the main union was sentenced to four years in a union funds case [C093].

> [I16]

---

<section_id: S11>
title: "Third arm: lawyers and civil society"
type: narrative
---

On 11 May 2024 masked plainclothes officers entered the Maison de l'Avocat and arrested the lawyer Sonia Dahmani live on France 24, over a remark on Carthage Plus [C094]. The police entered the same building again days later and arrested the lawyer Mehdi Zagrouba. The ICJ page says he reportedly showed physical signs of torture, his lawyers say he lost consciousness, and the ministry denies any mistreatment [C095]. We found no published outcome of an investigation into his complaint [C096].

In April 2026 the Tunisian League for Human Rights was notified of a one-month suspension. It was part of the quartet that won the 2015 Nobel Peace Prize. It called the decision arbitrary and went to court [C097].

---

<section_id: S12>
title: "Fourth arm: military justice"
type: narrative
---

In the three months after 25 July 2021 Amnesty recorded at least ten civilians before military courts, against six cases between 2011 and 2018 [C098]. Yassine Ayari, who invented the army story in 2011, went to prison on 28 July 2021 to serve a military sentence from 2018 over Facebook posts [C099]. In December 2023 the military court gave the opposition figure @chaima-issa a one-year suspended sentence [C100].

---

<section_id: S13>
title: "Fifth arm: Decree-Law 54"
type: narrative
---

By the end of 2023 Human Rights Watch counted about twenty people under criminal investigation under the decree [C101]. In February 2025 the journalists' union read a Court of Cassation ruling as limiting it to publishing cases, and in January 2026 it counted 28 files [C102]. Sonia Dahmani left prison on 27 November 2025 and in May 2026 was sentenced to two years over remarks about prisons [C103]. That same month the journalist @zied-el-heni was sentenced to a year under the Telecommunications Code, not the decree, for remarks at a seminar [C104].

In October 2025 a court went further. Saber Ben Chouchane, 51, was arrested in January 2024 and sentenced to death over Facebook posts [C105]. One charge was false news under Article 24 [C106]. After a campaign he was pardoned on 7 October. Human Rights Watch called it the first known death sentence in Tunisia over peaceful expression [C107].

---

<section_id: S14>
title: "The cases at the top"
type: narrative
---

The state-security conspiracy cases swept up opposition leaders. In February 2023 police arrested a series of opposition figures, and Rached Ghannouchi followed on 17 April [C108]. Inkyfada wrote that the accused were questioned on the basis of anonymous testimonies coded XX and XXX [C109]. In September 2026 the Court of Cassation rejected the last appeals, with sentences of up to 45 years [C110]. The building's own people were not spared either: @kamel-guizani, a former director general of the national police, was sentenced in absentia to 35 years in July 2025 [C111].

---

<section_id: S15>
title: "The body on the street"
type: narrative
---

All the arms reach the same body, the police on the street. The code of conduct bans torture outright [C112], but we found no published case of an officer sanctioned under it [C113]. The World Organisation Against Torture documented 21 police assaults from June 2024 to May 2025 [C114]. In the case of Omar Abidi, who drowned in 2018 while chased by police, the appeal court in July 2024 sentenced twelve officers to one year, suspended [C115].

---

<section_id: S16>
title: "Autumn 2026"
type: narrative
---

The arms are still moving. On 4 September 2026 Mohamed Yousfi, editor-in-chief of Al Katiba, was arrested, and on 8 September a detention order followed. Inkyfada reports the financial charges rest on a Facebook post and a comment, and that his name is not in them [C116]. After a demonstration for the Sumud flotilla activists, about fifteen people were arrested on 20 and 21 September, among them a minor [C117]. On 30 September the Official Gazette amended Article 24 of Decree-Law 54: a fine of 10,000 to 20,000 dinars, with prison only for repeat offences [C118].

Detention does not end at the police station. In the summer, AFP reported that at least 23 prisoners had reportedly died of heat. The authorities replied that no death can be attributed to heat without forensic confirmation, and published no figure [C119]. The League counts more than twenty, and an investigating judge took sixteen files [C120]. Lazhar Sta died in detention on 8 September, and his daughter alleges medical neglect. @abir-moussi's lawyer says she has been on hunger strike since 23 September, and Ahmed Nejib Chebbi says in a statement that he has been since 1 October [C121].

> [I17]

On 8 October Saied said accountability is a legitimate demand and that the truth will come out with no immunity for anyone [C122]. Article 110 of the 2022 constitution says the president cannot be held accountable for acts done in the course of his duties [C123].

---

<section_id: S17>
title: "Is this a police state?"
type: narrative
---

So, back to the central question. Did the police state Ben Ali built come back, or did it never leave?

> [I18]

The distance between the two eras is measurable in a single comparison. In 2002 Zouhair Yahyaoui was jailed on a false-news charge. In 2025 Saber Ben Chouchane was sentenced to death over Facebook posts [C124].

What does control of the building buy? The 2022 constitution limits the presidency to two terms in Article 90, but the 2014 lock on amendments that extend terms was removed [C125]. The decree of 30 September 2026 lets the president choose three members of the commission that oversees the 2029 elections [C126].

> [I19]

Nobody holds the building alone. One name we can point to is Khaled Yahyaoui, who by Tunisie Tribune's account took command of the ministry on the night of 25 July 2021 and who, according to Mondafrique in August 2026, was still head of presidential security [C127].

> [I20]

In 2011 the people brought down the head of the regime [C128].

> [I21]

title: "The Index"

If this ministry is what makes dictatorships and what everyone fights over the moment democracy appears, then the people need a way to measure their relationship with it. That is the Police Satisfaction Index, DeepTunisia's study 002. Its instrument takes about four minutes and asks for no name or email, and its method is published on the site [C129].

<section_id: S19>
title: "What this text does not say"
type: narrative
---

The film's voice said several things that the sources in our files do not carry. We left each out of this article.

- Yahyaoui's promotion to general in November 2021. We could not find it, and the film's script no longer contains it.
- The charge named for Seriati's reduced sentence, and the reason the torture charges against Kallel were dropped.
- The exact articles named for Ben Chouchane beyond Article 24.
- The phrase about record heat and power cuts in summer 2026, beyond the AFP report on prisoners.
- The 13 May date for the second raid on the Maison de l'Avocat.
- The stamp on the returned letter: sources say it came back marked no address, none mentions a stamp.

The film also gives 31 May 2022 for the decree appointing Saidane. That is the day he took office, and the decree itself is dated 17 June [C070].
